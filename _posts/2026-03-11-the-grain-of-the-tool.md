There is a phrase woodworkers use — *working with the grain* — that I keep returning to when I think about writing tools. A chisel taken against the grain tears and splinters. The same chisel, angled with it, moves through material that seems almost to want to be cut. The wood has a preferred direction. So does every tool.

Most writing software pretends it has no grain. The blank document, the infinite canvas, the interface that makes no demands. What could be more neutral? But neutrality is itself a design decision, and the blank page exerts its own pressure — the pressure of having no pressure at all. Facing it, I tend to reach for the first words that come, and those words tend to be approximate.

Tools with grain are more honest about what they are. A [fountain pen](https://www.jetpens.com/blog/the-fountain-pen-a-comprehensive-guide/pt/756) has a direction: you pull, rarely push, and the nib angle matters. The constraint is physical, inescapable, and educating. After a few hundred words you have learned something about how ink behaves on paper. You carry that knowledge even when you switch instruments.

---

## The Affordance as Argument

The design theorist Don Norman popularized the term *affordance* — the set of actions an object invites. A door handle affords pulling; a push plate affords pushing. But affordances are also arguments. Every affordance is implicitly a claim about what matters.

A word processor's affordance is the paragraph. Text is infinite; flow is the natural state; length is unconstrained. These are not neutral positions. They argue for a certain kind of writing: expansive, continuous, processual. Good for novels, perhaps. Less obviously good for thinking.

A constraint-based tool argues something different. Zettelkasten notecard software argues that ideas are atomic and should be sized to fit a single card. Twitter argued that an idea worth having could be made to fit in 140 characters. Neither claim is wholly right, but both produce writing you would not have written otherwise. The constraint is the editorial policy you didn't know you were following.

What I've come to believe: the best writing tools are ones whose grain you can feel, and therefore can work with or against *deliberately*. The trouble with frictionless tools is not that they're bad but that they make the grain invisible — and then you're at its mercy without knowing it.

---

## Friction as Information

When I write in a plain text file with no auto-formatting, no spell-check red squiggles, no suggested completions, what I lose in convenience I gain in a particular quality of attention. The rough surface makes me slow down. Slowing down, I notice where the argument has a soft spot, where I've used "therefore" where I should have used "however."

There's a version of this argument that romanticizes inconvenience, and I want to avoid it. A leaky pen is not more virtuous than one that works. The friction I'm describing isn't mere difficulty — it's *meaningful* difficulty, resistance that carries information about the material itself.

Consider the difference between writing on a screen and writing by hand. The screen affords infinite revision; handwriting makes every stroke relatively permanent. This is a practical disadvantage but a cognitive advantage. When deletion is costly, you commit to words more carefully before you write them. Commitment, it turns out, clarifies.

None of this means you should write everything by hand. It means you might want to notice what your tool makes easy and what it makes difficult, then decide whether those defaults align with what you're trying to do.

---

## A Practical Aside

If you want to experiment with this, a few approaches:

- Write a first draft in a medium that makes deletion slow — by hand, in a typewriter emulator, or in a markdown editor where you only allow yourself to move *forward*, not back-edit until the draft is complete.
- Use a timer. Twenty-five minutes of uninterrupted output. The time pressure is an artificial grain, but it works: it creates a direction.
- Write without formatting shortcuts. No headings until the second draft. The discipline of prose forces you to transition and connect rather than use a heading as a crutch.

These are not rules. They're ways of making the grain visible. Once you can feel it, you can decide what to do with it.

---

The best tools I've used have something like a personality — not an interface personality, all rounded corners and friendly microcopy, but an *aesthetic* personality. The way a good knife has a right way to hold it that reveals itself after an hour of use. Writing tools that have no grain at all make me vaguely uneasy, the same way a room with no shadows does. What am I not seeing?

I'm still looking for the writing tool that shows me the grain of my own thinking. I suspect it doesn't exist as software. I suspect it's mostly a habit of noticing.
